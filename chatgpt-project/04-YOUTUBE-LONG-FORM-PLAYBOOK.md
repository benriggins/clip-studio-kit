# YouTube long-form playbook

*Use this in Mode C (long-form) and whenever the user asks for titles, thumbnails or a video plan. It works for any
niche: swap the examples for the creator's own topic from `06-BRAND-PROFILE.md`.*

No title, thumbnail, script formula or retention number guarantees a viral video. What the evidence does support is
a repeatable way to package, structure and review videos. That is what this file is.

---

## 1. What makes a long-form video spread

YouTube describes performance in three parts:

1. **Appeal: will they click?** The topic, title, thumbnail, relevance and perceived value.
2. **Engagement: will they keep watching?** The first 30 seconds, storytelling, demonstrations, pacing, and value
   that keeps arriving.
3. **Satisfaction: was it worth it?** The promise delivered, usefulness, trust, and a reason to come back.

Recommendations also depend on each viewer's interests and history, competing videos, and demand for the topic.

A useful working model (a planning tool, **not** YouTube's ranking formula):

> **Audience demand × packaging × retention × satisfaction × shareability**

If any factor is close to zero, the video struggles: a brilliant thumbnail on a weak video, a great video nobody
clicks, or a great video on a topic too few people care about.

**The core move:** turn the topic into a specific, human outcome.
- Weak: "Understanding Time Management"
- Strong: "I Planned Every Hour for 30 Days. Here's What Actually Changed."

The strong version has a recognisable situation, an experiment, a visible result, and a question the viewer wants
answered.

## 2. The first 30 seconds

YouTube reports how many viewers are still watching at 30 seconds and advises making the opening match the title
and thumbnail. It groups videos that keep 50%+ at that point as "above typical" intros. That's a reporting
reference, not a viral threshold.

A 2026 third-party analysis of 349 videos across 36 channels (observational, not causal) found that videos which
beat their channel's usual views more often **addressed the title's promise** and made a **concrete claim**
early. Context dumps were more common in underperformers. Notably, **speaking speed barely differed** between
winners and losers. Give viewers something specific; don't just cut every breath.

### A 30-second structure (a starting point; the parts can overlap)

| Time | Job | What it sounds like |
|---|---|---|
| 0–3 s | **Proof or a provocative claim** | Show the result, or name a problem they instantly recognise. |
| 3–8 s | **Personal stakes** | Why it matters to their time, money, work or identity. |
| 8–15 s | **Tension** | The surprise, trade-off, mistake or open question. |
| 15–23 s | **Clear promise** | Exactly what they'll learn or be able to do. |
| 23–30 s | **Proof and transition** | Credibility, then straight into the content. No intro. |

**Cut openings like this:** "Hey everyone, welcome back. Today we're going to dive into some really exciting…"
It's generic, slow, and could start a thousand other videos.

**Open like this:** "I tried three ways to plan my week on the same project. One saved the most time. Another
looked the best. But the one I kept wasn't either of them."

A hook can create suspense, but **the video must resolve it**. Withholding the answer or manufacturing drama
hurts satisfaction.

**Mode C rule:** you're cutting a recording, not writing a script. Build the opening from the **strongest truthful
lines the speaker actually said**, moved to the front if needed. If no line does the job, add it to the pickup list
with the exact words to record.

## 3. Titles: sell the transformation, not the topic

A title has two jobs: make the right viewer recognise it's for them, and give them a reason to click. Keep it
accurate and concise, with the important words first.

| Formula | Example |
|---|---|
| Experiment + outcome | I Let ___ Plan My Week. Here's What Happened. |
| Mistake + correction | Why Your ___ Keeps Failing |
| Time-boxed mastery | Learn ___ in 15 Minutes |
| Identity + transformation | How to Become ___ Without Losing ___ |
| Unexpected contrast | ___ Can Do the Task. It Can't Make the Decision. |
| Practical proof | I Built a ___ From Scratch |

Match the viewer's awareness. Beginners search for clear instructions; experienced viewers browsing their feed
respond to an experiment, a surprise or a challenged belief.

**Every time, write three candidates:**
- **Search-first:** "How to Build a Weekly Planning System"
- **Curiosity-first:** "I Stopped Planning My Week Myself"
- **Outcome-first:** "My Planning System: What Worked and What Failed"

Recommend the one the video **actually delivers**. Never choose the most sensational title if the opening and
payoff can't support it.

## 4. Thumbnails: one idea, seen in a second

YouTube says 90% of its best-performing videos use custom thumbnails (a correlation, not proof of cause).
- **One focal point.** Text (if any) readable at phone size, 1–4 words.
- **Add information; don't repeat the title.** Title: "I Built a Second Brain for My Business". Thumbnail text:
  "WOULD I HIRE IT?"
- Prefer something **real from the video**: a screenshot, a result, an object, a genuine expression.
- Four directions that work across niches: **proof and surprise** ("IT WORKED?"), **before/after**, **human
  tension** ("WHO DECIDES?"), and **clarity** ("START HERE").

YouTube Studio can A/B test up to three title and thumbnail combinations on eligible long-form videos. It picks
winners by **watch time**, not clicks. So package for the people who'll enjoy the whole video, not people who'll
feel misled.

## 5. After the first 30 seconds

The hook earns the next 30 seconds. The content has to earn the rest. For a typical 10–15-minute video (there is
no universal ideal length; use what the topic needs, without padding):

| Section | Job |
|---|---|
| 0:00–0:30 | Hook, stakes, promise, tension |
| 0:30–1:30 | Show the real example or preview the result: prove the promise is real |
| 1:30–3:00 | The problem, the criteria and the approach |
| 3:00–7:00 | Build, test, teach or compare: the core value |
| 7:00–10:00 | Findings, complications and improvements: the deeper payoff |
| Final section | Recap the decisions, give next steps, point to the next video |

### Ten things strong videos include
1. **A real artifact:** the actual document, result, dashboard, product or before/after.
2. **A human perspective:** what you expected, what happened, and why you decided what you did.
3. **A visible transformation:** show it, don't just say it.
4. **A teaching framework:** a memorable way to understand and apply the lesson.
5. **Specific examples:** a believable scenario, not abstractions.
6. **Progression:** levels, stages or experiments, so each section moves the story on.
7. **Real trade-offs:** where it fails, costs too much or needs supervision.
8. **Useful visual changes:** talking head, on-screen evidence, diagrams and demos *where they clarify*.
9. **A reproducible takeaway:** steps, a template, criteria or a checklist.
10. **A related next video:** guide them to the natural next lesson.

**Pattern interrupts must add information, not just movement.** Zooms, captions, stock footage and sound effects
don't replace a clear explanation. This is why explainers should show what the words can't (see `01-SOP.md` §6).

## 6. Topic selection (you can't edit your way out of a weak idea)

Three kinds of videos, each with a different job:
- **Search videos:** solve a defined problem people type into YouTube ("How to ___ without ___").
- **Discovery videos:** an experiment interesting enough to reach people who weren't searching ("I Let ___ Run
  My ___ for a Day").
- **Authority videos:** the creator's perspective, which is why viewers come back ("The Decisions I'll Never
  Outsource").

A reasonable starting mix (a hypothesis to test, not a rule): about **50% search, 30% discovery, 20% authority**.

**Before recording, an idea should pass five questions:**
1. Is there existing demand or a recognisable problem?
2. Does it appeal beyond people already obsessed with the topic?
3. Can we show a visible result or real evidence?
4. Is there a genuinely new insight, test or improvement?
5. Would people who liked the previous videos want this?

When studying other channels, compare each video to **that channel's own median**, not raw view counts.

## 7. Reading the numbers after publishing

| You see | It may mean | Look at |
|---|---|---|
| Low CTR, strong retention | Good video, weak packaging | Title, thumbnail, topic appeal |
| High CTR, sharp drop in the first 30 s | The opening doesn't match the promise | The hook; show proof sooner |
| Strong first minute, big dip mid-video | Slow setup or a weak section | Exact drop-off timestamps |
| Strong CTR and retention, few impressions | Narrow demand or heavy competition | Topic breadth, audience |
| Good views, few returning viewers | The topic worked; the channel's promise didn't | Series, consistency |

These are diagnoses to investigate, not certain explanations. YouTube's CTR guidance says half of channels and
videos fall between 2% and 10% (a range, not a target). Don't judge a video on early CTR wobbles. A/B tests can
take up to two weeks.

**48-hour and 7-day review:** CTR by traffic source (Home vs Suggested) · % still watching at 30 s · dips and
replayed moments · average view duration vs similar-length videos · new vs returning viewers · comments (missing
explanations, next video ideas) · A/B results · **one improvement for the next video**.

## 8. Staying original

Firsthand evidence and judgment can't be duplicated by someone generating another script. Show real work, real
experiments, real results and your own analysis. Separate evidence from forecasts.

YouTube's monetization rules treat repetitive, mass-produced material as inauthentic content. AI-assisted
production is allowed, but generic videos at scale can cause monetization problems. Use YouTube's disclosure
setting for realistic synthetic people, places or events. YouTube says the label alone doesn't hurt
recommendations.

Every video can answer three questions; the third is what makes viewers choose **this** creator:

| Layer | The viewer's question |
|---|---|
| Capability | What's possible? |
| Application | How do I use it in my real life or work? |
| Judgment | *Should* I, when, and what stays my responsibility? |

## 9. A 30-day validation plan (when the user is starting a channel)

- **Week 1:** two videos in different formats (a tutorial and an experiment), three title/thumbnail concepts each.
- **Week 2:** two more, improving the first 30 seconds and showing evidence earlier.
- **Week 3:** follow up within the most promising topic: a deeper case study or comparison.
- **Week 4:** two connected videos, then pick next month's formats from demand, retention and response.

Eight videos is too small a sample for a universal formula. It's a starting point.

## 10. The video brief (fill this in for every long-form plan)

| Field | Answer |
|---|---|
| Audience | Who exactly is this for? |
| Problem or desire | What do they want or struggle with? |
| Transformation | What changes for them after watching? |
| Title | The recommended one of three |
| Thumbnail | One idea; text that adds to the title |
| First 30 seconds | The exact transcript lines, in order |
| Evidence | What in the video proves the promise |
| Final payoff | What resolves the hook |

If the video can't answer audience, transformation, evidence and payoff, say so, and say what's missing (often a
pickup the creator can record).

A format that works in almost any niche:
**a compelling question → a real experiment → an unexpected insight → a usable framework → an honest conclusion.**
